Methodology

How Pomop decides what you should do next

Pomop scores every open task on five weighted factors — urgency (45%), importance (26%), time fit (15%), workload impact (10%), and task age (4%) — and recommends whichever scores highest.

A task list can tell you what exists. It doesn't necessarily tell you what deserves attention right now. Pomop's decision engine turns that list into one recommendation, calculated from the task data you've entered. It doesn't use an AI model.

Written by Independent Product Builder · Last updated October 22, 2026

The five factors

Every open task is scored on five factors. None of them decides the outcome alone.

Urgency

Based on how close a task's deadline is. Pomop's implementation uses fixed bands: overdue is the most urgent, followed by due within 24 hours, due in 1–3 days, due in 3–7 days, and due in 7 or more days. A task with no deadline is treated as the least urgent — an undated task never outranks a dated one on urgency alone.

Importance

The priority level you set on a task — low, medium, high, or critical — mapped to an evenly spaced scale. If a task's importance is missing or invalid, it defaults to medium, never to critical, so incomplete task data can't accidentally dominate the ranking.

Time fit

Whether a task's estimated remaining time fits in the time you have right now. A task that fits gets full credit, whether it needs 10 minutes of a 25-minute window or all 25. A task that needs more time than you have isn't excluded — it's recommended as a partial session, and it scores the share this session would cover (25 minutes of a 100-minute task is a quarter). A task with no time estimate gets a neutral middle score, so leaving the estimate out neither helps nor hurts it.

Workload impact

How much of a task's remaining work one session would clear. A 20-minute task in a 25-minute session gets full credit because you'd finish it. A 2-hour task gets about a fifth, because 25 minutes would only dent it. A task with no time estimate gets a neutral middle score.

Task age

How long a task has been sitting on your list. The score grows evenly from nothing on the day you add a task to its full 4% at 30 days, then stops growing. Age works as a tiebreaker: when two tasks are otherwise close, the one that has waited longer comes first. It adds at most 0.04 to a score. That's less than one step of importance (low to medium is worth 0.065) and less than the smallest step between deadline bands (0.045), so waiting longer never outranks a higher priority or a nearer deadline.

The scoring model

The current implementation weighs the five factors as follows:

FactorWeight
Urgency45%
Importance26%
Time fit15%
Workload impact10%
Task age4%

These weights are a product decision, not a scientifically proven formula. They're set so that no single attribute decides the recommendation on its own.

Why not just priority?

A task can be marked high importance and still not be the best thing to work on right now. It can become more relevant for reasons that have nothing to do with how important it was originally marked: its deadline is approaching, it fits the time you actually have available, it's been waiting a long time, or working on it now would meaningfully reduce your workload. Pomop's decision engine combines all of these rather than sorting on any single one.

Deterministic, not AI

The recommendation is produced through a fixed scoring calculation, not an AI model. Given the same tasks, the same available time, and the same moment, Pomop always returns the same recommendation, and every step of the calculation is described on this page. That doesn't make it right every time. It means that when you disagree with a pick, you can see which input caused it.

"Why this task?"

Every recommendation comes with plain-language reasons, like "Due today" or "High importance." These reasons come from the same factors used to calculate the score, so they can't drift away from it. If a reason doesn't apply to a task (for example, a task with no deadline has no urgency-based reason), it doesn't appear. Nothing is generated; each reason is a readout of one scoring input.

What the recommendation doesn't know

The decision engine only ever sees the task data you enter. It has no way to account for:

  • your personal energy or focus level at a given moment
  • context or importance that isn't reflected in a task's priority or deadline
  • unexpected events that come up outside the app
  • dependencies between tasks — it doesn't know if one task is blocked on another
  • other people's priorities, deadlines, or work — Pomop only sees your own task list

When Pomop may not be the best fit

Pomop is built around one person's own task list, not shared work. It's likely a weaker fit for:

  • team or collaborative project management, where priorities depend on other people's work
  • heavily meeting-driven schedules, where a calendar — not a task list — is the primary source of truth
  • tasks with complex dependencies that need to be sequenced, not just ranked
  • environments where priorities are set and change outside of what's recorded in the app

Privacy

Your task data — titles, deadlines, priorities, and progress — is stored only in your browser's localStorage, with no account and no backend that receives or processes it. This is separate from website analytics: Pomop uses Google Analytics to understand aggregate site usage, and analytics cookies are only set if you accept the cookie banner. It's a separate, third-party service, and it never receives your tasks. See the full Privacy Policy for details.
